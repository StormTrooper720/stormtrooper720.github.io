I would say it all started when my parents got me LEGOs. I loved building things out of them. I'd often build the set, then take it apart or add to it. Even now, I only have a few completed sets with bins of LEGOs waiting to be turned into something cool. Soon after, I got a snap circuits kit. I would make every project it had in the book. From flashing lights, to an AM radio transmitter. I didn't want to stop building, I was hooked.

Eventually, I got gifted an Arduino starter kit from my grandparents. I didn't know what to do with it at first and it mostly sat untouched until I learned about programming. I was at a bookstore with my family and I saw a book called "Python Crash Course" by Eric Matthes. It was a guide to learning the Python programming language. Everything from outputting "Hello World!" in the terminal, to making games and websites. I convinced my parents that $40 was worth it and when I got home, I immediately started following along on the family computer.

Soon, I reached the point where I made the space invaders project from the book. It felt amazing that I could make a computer do all these things, it was only my imagination that was the limit. Around the same time, I got the LEGO Mindstorms kit and made a Rubik's cube solver project. It was super cool building, programming, and seeing something I made right in front of my eyes.

But then I remembered about the Arduino kit. I grabbed it off my shelf and opened it for the first time. It looked intimidating. All these sensors, wires, lights, displays, and of course, the brains of the kit – a microcontroller. What do they do? How do they work? How can I make something out of them?

I downloaded the Arduino IDE and followed the included booklet with examples on how to use each component. I didn't realize it then, but I was learning an entirely new programming language, C++. It was definitely different from Python and took some time to get comfortable with, but I was able to make some cool circuits.

When I was roughly 11 years old, I got a Raspberry Pi 3. This was the first computer I got that was actually mine, I didn't have to share it with my sister. It had way less power than the family computer so I still played video games on that, but all of my programming from then on occurred on the Pi.

It ran Linux though, an operating system vastly different than Windows. What made it worse was that it used an ARM processor, even today computer software doesn't prioritize ARM and the workarounds to make it run aren't the best. Still, I figured out how to code on it and even hooked components and sensors directly up to its pins to control them.

For Christmas, I got my first actual desktop computer. I would no longer have to struggle with the intricate workings of Linux on an ARM processor to try and run Minecraft. But Windows felt limiting. Yes, it looked nicer, ran any app or game in existence, and caused minimal issues but I couldn't mess with it how I liked.

At some point I kept getting blue screens from driver issues. It turns out I could no longer use the latest NVIDIA drivers and keep my system stable. At that time I was also trying to learn C++ to make apps outside of my Arduino, but I kept struggling with the bloated Visual Studio and the seemingly neglected development experience on Windows.

I installed Linux on my main PC. After looking up many peoples' opinions on which distribution to use (there are hundreds of options, each with their own use case), I settled with PopOS. First I dual booted, but kept finding myself going to PopOS instead. Eventually I overwrote my Windows install with it and never looked back.

I started high school and took all the computer science and engineering classes I could. In hindsight, I should’ve waited to take those so that I wouldn’t be taking P.E. classes with a bunch of freshman my junior year but I digress. I learned JavaScript, website design, SolidWorks, and many other things. Senior year, I actually received my SolidWorks professionals certification.

Sophomore year, one of my friends told me to join the Technology Student Association (TSA). He said he got an old drone from a garage sale and that there was a competition that involved building a drone. It should be an easy task then? Nope.

Many years prior, my dad got a 3D printer so he could physically show people the infrastructure models he helped design. But outside of printing random things, I never really thought about its potential. Until I started designing this new quad copter.

My dad’s 3D printer sat idle for years waiting for a purpose after he no longer needed it for his job. I downloaded the software, turned the printer on, loaded the filament, and printed my very own model. It was crazy to think I could design a part, and see a machine make it only hours later. I designed and printed a new drone frame, only to figure out I messed up the dimensions pretty badly. I printed a new design.

I was way over my head in terms of the scope of the project. There was issue upon issue and I had no clue about how to make it work. I didn’t give up though, I kept pushing to figure out how to make a functioning drone with a claw on the bottom. Yes, the competition involved picking up small objects and transporting them with the drone. My team showed up to the state competition, only for our drone to become unstable and fly into the net, twice.

During sophomore year as well, I took a robotics class. The assignments centered around making a robot move certain ways depending on the sensors it came equipped with. At the end of the first semester, the final for the class was to navigate a maze, follow a line around obstacles, and stop exactly on the finish line. All without touching the robot, your code was in control. Easy enough. Although I had to overcome a few issues, I was able to reuse a lot of previous code from the class.

Time for the second semester of the class, build your own robot. From planning to finished result, my grade was tied solely to making a robot that functioned autonomously. How about a robot that plays Connect Four? Shouldn’t be too hard? Once again I was wrong.

Using a cheap laser engraver I got for free, I repurposed the motors, belts, and frame to build a contraption around an unmodified Connect Four board. Adding a Raspberry Pi, camera, and custom designed piece dropper, the machine was complete. Except for having to modify the piece dropper, fix wiring, and actually completing the logic among other things.

It’s easy to design a part, find components, and usually put them together, its making them work together that’s the problem. You can screw a motor in and connect it to the power board, but making it turn, especially only when you tell it to, and consistently, it’s almost never happens first try. After a lot of time, I figured out how to make everything work together and the machine worked.

It wasn’t the best, wasn’t reliable, didn’t look good, wasn’t fast, and was very unreliable (Wait, I already said that…). It accomplished the goal though, of autonomously playing Connect Four against an opponent without human intervention. Most of the time, half the time, occasionally, a few times, when the sun was at the right angle (not even joking, the camera sucked). In short, it broke a lot but it was cool and I easily got an A.

My remaining years of high school, I continued working on a drone for the same competition. Even as my team went from around five members sophomore year to me and an emotional support friend senior year, I kept making improvement after improvement. It got 1st place in the district senior year and should’ve placed higher in the state competition but I won’t get into why here.

I’ve continued to work on projects throughout high school for fun. I love the challenges I face when working on something, even if I hate them in the moment. It’s how I learn, improve, and even get into a new field of engineering. I never thought I’d make a fully custom drone, make a robot that plays Connect Four on its own, make my first video game, and even make a simple AM radio transmitter. The “I actually did that?!” feeling is only the side effect of finishing the project, the main effect is learning something new and trying something you never would’ve done before.

Now I’m going into college, kind of nervous, kind of excited. My goal will be to learn something new every day and apply it. Try something new every day, even if it doesn’t work out. Inside and outside of the classroom I will continue to push myself and accomplish something bigger than before.

As I’m writing this, I’m also redesigning my website. Although I’m using a template, the writing is still my own. While writing isn’t exactly engineering, I’m still getting the “I actually did that?!” feeling of finishing this story and making my website look better. I’ve never written a story of my life like this, so I’m still learning how to lay it out and make it at least somewhat easy to read. This form of writing is new to me, the drone was new to me, everything at some point in my life was never before done by me until I tried it. All I’m trying to say is that you should go out there and try something new, something you never would’ve done before. You would be surprised at what you can accomplish, I know I still am.

--Trooper Gothard
